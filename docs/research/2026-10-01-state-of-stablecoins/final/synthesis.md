## 1. What this draft corrects, and why it matters

The upstream passes and both parent reviews were treated as leads, not as verdicts. Re-opening the
stored primary captures changed the answer in eight places. Two of these corrections overturn a
conclusion a review had already asserted as settled.

### 1.1 The Kansas City Fed arithmetic disagrees with itself, and both reviews missed it

The conditional Treasury-demand example is central to the US-debt section, so it needs to be exact.
The stored capture states:

> Under the strong assumption that banks' and issuers' current asset mixes continue to hold, an
> additional $1 in stablecoins would increase total Treasury holdings by $0.50. The corresponding $1
> less in bank deposits would decrease Treasury holdings by $0.08 cents and decrease loans by $0.50
> (the remaining $0.42 is held in other assets). In this scenario, the net effect of $1 moved from
> banks to a stablecoin issuer would still decrease bank lending by around $0.50 but increase total
> Treasury holdings by $0.30.

Those are the source's own figures. Tool arithmetic: 0.50 - 0.08 = 0.42, not 0.30. The article
states 0.30; its own components give 0.42; the gap is 0.12 and the article offers no reconciliation
(SS-001, SS-002). **The parent review did not catch this.** It challenged a different number in the
same passage, the child's fabricated "0.08 quote" framing, and then accepted the article's stated
0.30 as correct.

The version conflict is broader than one coefficient (SS-005). PDF reader text gives a 20 percent
bank Treasury share, a 0.20 Treasury reduction and 1 percent loan contraction. Revised HTML gives
8 percent, 0.08 and 2.5 percent, and cites 2026 Federal Reserve data. Both retain a 0.30 net.
The HTML explicitly identifies a September 22, 2026 publisher correction, so it is the later
documented revision. The PDF reader is truncated, with unknown cache freshness, and cannot
establish a competing current version. The HTML's parenthetical 0.42 describes other bank assets,
not an explicit correction of its stated net Treasury demand. Its net-demand arithmetic still
does not reconcile. The dollar/cents wording adds unit ambiguity. Intended net-demand coefficient
remains UNRESOLVED; no headline rests on either version.

The source's hedge is the more important point for the narrative, and it survives all of this:

> should the sources of the funds that are shifted toward stablecoins sell Treasuries at the same
> rate that stablecoin issuers purchase them, then a larger stablecoin market will have no net effect
> on Treasury demand at all (SS-003)

And it names a case where the effect reverses sign: households forgoing or selling Treasury purchases
would *reduce* Treasury demand, since $1 fewer household purchase creates only $0.50 of issuer
purchase (SS-004). Gross issuer Treasury holdings are therefore not net new Treasury demand. The
direction of the effect depends on where the money comes from, and the source says so itself.

### 1.2 GENIUS reserves are not T-bills only, and the statute text was sitting in the record

The overbroad claim is corrected against the enrolled text, stored at
`final/evidence/congress-genius-act-text.json`. Section 4(a)(1)(A) requires reserves at least
1 to 1 comprising eight classes: coins and currency; insured deposits; Treasury bills, notes or bonds
of 93 days or less (whether remaining maturity or original maturity); overnight repo backed by such
bills; overnight reverse repo against Treasury notes, bills or bonds, tri-party, centrally cleared or
bilateral with a creditworthy counterparty; shares of a registered Government money market fund
invested solely in the preceding classes; other similarly liquid Federal Government-issued assets
approved by the regulator; and tokenized forms of the eligible classes (SS-010).

The 93-day figure the earlier drafts flagged as uncorroborated secondary reporting is in fact in the
statute. What was wrong was not the number but the framing: reserves are not bound to Treasury bills,
they are bound to a list in which Treasury bills are one of several categories.

Two further statutory duties are on the record: monthly published reserve composition including
average tenor and geographic location of custody per category (SS-011), and a prohibition on
pledging or rehypothecating reserves outside the statute's exceptions (SS-012). Section 3(g)
separately denies non-permitted stablecoins the treatment of cash or cash equivalent for accounting
purposes, cash-equivalent margin and collateral eligibility, and acceptable wholesale settlement
asset status (SS-013).

### 1.3 The IMF $300 billion figure was relabelled as Treasury demand. It is not.

The figure belongs to IMF Working Paper WP/26/52, and it measures lost market value of listed
incumbent payment firms:

> we derive a back-of-the-envelope estimate that the passage of the GENIUS Act in 2025 reduced the
> total market capitalization of incumbent payment firms by 18% or approximately $300 billion, with a
> range of $220-470 billion (SS-020)

Two things follow. First, this is an equity-market valuation result about payment companies. It is
not a measure of Treasury demand, and describing it as one conflates two unrelated quantities. Second,
it is a scaled estimate, not a directly observed total: the paper divides an observed relative price
move by the change in prediction-market probability around the decisive vote (SS-021). The paper
itself calls it back-of-the-envelope, and the authors label working papers as research in progress.

The Treasury-yield result comes from a *different* paper, WP/26/44, which finds that stablecoin demand
shocks triggered persistent declines in short-term Treasury yields and a dollar depreciation, with
payment providers benefiting and banks showing no evidence of priced disintermediation risk (SS-022).
Conflating the two papers produced the original error.

Tool recomputation of the scaling gives -18.57 percent from the paper's stated components against its
rounded 18 percent. That is ordinary rounding within a method the paper already calls rough, not a
defect, and it is recorded rather than corrected.

### 1.4 Open USD does not automatically give holders legal ownership of reserves

The planted claim in the challenge fixture was that holding OUSD gives each holder direct legal title to
the Treasury securities and the right to instruct BlackRock's trades. No capture supports it, and the
captures actively cut against it. What is documented is:

- OUSD is issued by Bridge Building Inc., and went live on Base, Ethereum, Solana and Tempo
  (SS-052).
- Open Standard's own launch page states that OUSD is issued by Bridge, a Stripe company, with
  reserves held at BlackRock, Lead Bank and BNY (SS-052b). Two separate issuer-side captures are
  used here because neither alone states both the legal entity and the corporate parent, and the
  "a Stripe company" phrase is not asserted from the Bridge post.
- Bridge states it received conditional OCC approval to establish Bridge National Trust Bank (SS-054).
  No primary OCC order is stored in this record, so the conditions and current status are unverified
  here, and conditional approval is not a final charter.
- Reserve earnings accrue to *partners*, not token holders. Open Standard states that founders and
  participating partners have the opportunity to earn equity based on supply and activity they drive
  on their platforms (SS-050). Holder yield is a different thing from partner economics and the two
  must never be collapsed, which is precisely the error the planted claim made.

So a holder has a claim against the issuer. That is not a direct proprietary claim on reserve assets,
and nothing in this record establishes an instruction right over how reserves are invested. The reserve
assets are not even Treasuries alone: the reserve page reported $257,214,691 cash and $211,230,708
Treasuries against total supply of $468,445,399 (SS-053). Tool check: the two components sum exactly to
total supply, and the composition is 54.91 percent cash, 45.09 percent Treasuries.

### 1.5 Open USD's yield-sharing is NOT structurally unique. Circle already does this.

This is the correction with the widest consequence, because it undercuts the central promotional claim.
Earlier drafts asserted that sharing reserve earnings with partners is a fundamental departure from the
conventional model. Circle's own Q2 2026 Form 10-Q, stored at `final/evidence/circle-crcl-20260630-10q.txt`,
refutes that:

> our distribution costs payable to key distributors such as Coinbase and Binance are directly
> impacted by the amount of USDC held on their respective platforms (SS-060)

Circle ties distributor economics to balances held. The filings quantify it:

| Line (USD thousands) | 3 months to 2026-06-30 | 6 months to 2026-06-30 |
|---|---|---|
| Reserve income | 667,733 | 1,320,241 |
| Distribution and transaction costs | 410,414 | 815,816 |
| Ratio (computed) | 0.6146 | 0.6179 |

Accrued distribution costs stood at 105,799 thousand as of June 30, 2026, down from 119,038 thousand
at December 31, 2025 (SS-062), which is 8.01 percent of the six-month reserve income (computed).

The filing combines distribution and transaction costs in that headline line. However, a separate
passage discloses Coinbase distribution costs of $324.6 million for the quarter and $655.3 million
for the half year. It also discloses Coinbase allocations based on platform USDC after issuer
retention, plus half the remaining broader-ecosystem amount after approved third-party allocations
(SS-064). R3's claim that no formula or separate distributor costs were disclosed was too broad.
This is a named Coinbase arrangement, not a universal rate. The retention amount and comparable
Open Standard contractual terms remain UNRESOLVED; generosity cannot yet be compared.

What can be said is an **inference**, and only this (SS-100): Open Standard's proposal to route
reserve earnings to partners is a redistribution of an existing industry practice, not its
introduction. Its novelty claim is not established by the evidence in this record. Whether Open
Standard is more or less generous is UNRESOLVED: partial Circle terms are now evidenced, but a
like-for-like Open Standard contract and realized payments are still missing.

### 1.6 The x402 volume range is a scope problem, and two earlier challenges were themselves wrong

Headline x402 value measurements in this record span roughly $974 thousand to $2.59 billion, and the
ranges cannot be reconciled by subtraction. The figures have different chain sets, date windows,
denominators and adjustment rules, and the correct response is to present them side by side with
their scopes rather than to average or subtract them.

Three specific corrections:

- **Bitquery reports a $317 million five-chain residual after removing one Arbitrum contract.**
  It gives Arbitrum volume as $2.33 billion and the contract share as 97.5 percent (SS-081).
  Subtracting that contract from the containing five-chain $2.59 billion total is valid:
  rounded inputs give $318.25 million, close to but not exactly the reported $317 million.
  Unrounded inputs are not stored. The earlier $64.75 million challenge incorrectly applied
  Arbitrum's share to every chain. The continuation reviewer also called subset removal a
  scope error; that interpretation is rejected. The residual includes the other chains and
  is neither Arbitrum-only nor commerce-only. Original arithmetic labels were misleading
  and are replaced by explicitly scoped computations in `final/evidence/arithmetic.json`.
- **Bitquery's cross-chain shape**: five countably comparable EVM chains settled 18.3 million payments
  worth 2.6 billion in August 2026, with almost nine tenths of that value in a single bridging contract (SS-080).
- **The low end is now evidenced rather than asserted.** The earlier draft put the bottom of the range at
  $996 thousand across 18.1 million transactions; that figure came from a prose research draft and is
  withdrawn, because it matches neither the volume nor the transaction count of any stored capture. The
  evidenced low point is the x402scan dashboard's own cumulative total as fetched on 2026-10-01:
  12,293,450 transactions and 974,215,144,043 raw USDC units, which is $974,215.14 at six decimals (SS-085).
  That dashboard's window and inclusion rules are not stated in the capture, so its total still cannot be
  placed on the same axis as an August-only five-chain count.

Corroboration on the substance of the bot problem, from independent firms:

- Bitquery: two automated loops account for 83 percent of the agent payments found, one wallet sending
  13.2 million payments to a single address over 52 days on Base, plus 5.7 million one-cent payments on
  Polygon (SS-082).
- Chainalysis: growth was driven substantially by meme coin farming, with PING, a pay-to-mint
  experiment charging 1 USDC, processing over 150,000 transactions in its first month (SS-083).
- Visa and Artemis, on a wash-adjusted basis as of April 2026: about $15.0 million across 109.6
  million transactions since the May 2025 launch (SS-084).

Note that the Visa and Artemis pair sits inside the Bitquery range while being scoped very differently:
a wash-adjusted, single-ecosystem, as-of-April measure. It corroborates that value and transaction
count can diverge sharply, which is the point being made here. It does not average with the others.

The measured samples establish bridge traffic, automated loops and protocol experimentation. They
do not establish one market-wide non-commerce share. The true commerce share remains UNRESOLVED:
no source in this record estimates a common commerce-only denominator. Narrative: x402 is a payment
mechanism being used, but these totals do not yet demonstrate broad goods-and-services commerce.
Removing bridges and known bots alone would not prove the residual is commerce; that requires
merchant/service attribution and treatment of tests, refunds, repeat loops and self-payments.

### 1.7 The 95-to-99 percent non-payment claim was arithmetically invented

An earlier draft asserted that 95 to 99 percent of stablecoin volume is trading and protocol activity.
That figure appears nowhere in any stored capture. The BCG and Allium white paper that the draft leaned
on actually reports two different things, and the draft used only the smaller one:

> Public blockchain data suggests there is more than $62 trillion of stablecoin transfers annually, but
> our analysis reveals that real economic activity amounts to just $4.2 trillion, or about 7% of the
> total (SS-030)

and separately

> in 2025, there was approximately $350-$550 billion of observable bilateral payments for goods and
> services. We exclude large categories of off-chain usage, including internal exchange settlements and
> stablecoin card-based payments. As such, the numbers should be interpreted as directionally robust
> lower bounds (SS-031)

Tool arithmetic: 4.2 / 62 = 6.77 percent, consistent with the source's "about 7 percent" (SS-030).
The bilateral figure is 0.565 to 0.887 percent of the gross total, a much narrower measure defined as
goods-and-services payments with named exclusions.

The correct framing: real economic activity is on the order of 7 percent of gross transfers under the
broad definition, and under 1 percent under a narrow goods-and-services definition the source itself
labels a lower bound. The 95-to-99 percent claim is excluded outright.

### 1.8 BUIDL AUM and the "idle value" definition remain open

No primary issuer disclosure of BUIDL assets under management is stored in this record. What is
stored is three differently dated secondary figures for one fund, and none of them is interchangeable
with another:

- $2.5 to $2.9 billion, and tradeable on Uniswap via UniswapX since February 2026 (SS-072, aggregator
  reporting, as of early July 2026).
- about $2.7 billion, alongside Circle USYC at about $3.0 billion (SS-072b, a separate compilation
  citing DefiLlama, as of August 2026). This same compilation elsewhere brackets both funds as
  "each ~$2.4-3.0 billion", which contains rather than contradicts the 2.7 billion point figure.
- approximately $2.4 billion as of May 2026, deployed across eight named chains (SS-072c, secondary
  research-house reporting).

The second of these is also where an earlier draft's attribution was wrong. That draft cited the
aggregator capture for the $2.7 billion figure; that capture contains no such figure, and its only
"2.7" is a $2.7 trillion 2030 DeFi-deployment projection, a different quantity in a different unit
(SS-072). **No current BUIDL AUM figure is asserted here.** The gap is retained, not closed.

The publisher's own article and report landing page correct R3's interpretation (SS-071). Of 1,289
tested assets above $100,000, 910 recorded zero weekly transfers and 379 recorded movement. The
zero-transfer count share computes to 70.60 percent. The reported 56 percent is value-weighted:
$32.9 billion without transfers versus $26.2 billion active, or 55.67 percent using rounded inputs.
The landing page separately dates its data to May 31, 2026 and gives a $60 billion market base
(SS-103). Against that base, $32.9 billion is 54.83 percent, not the 55.67 percent reproduced
against the article's $59.1 billion transfer-measured subset. The bases are not interchangeable.
The precise transfer week and exclusions remain
UNRESOLVED. About $27 billion of the dormant value came from Represented assets, which the publisher
says were not necessarily designed for public secondary-market movement.

This establishes reported low weekly transfer activity, not economically idle capital. The full
report and underlying dataset are not stored. Narrative: headline tokenized value overstates the
size of a publicly moving market, while ledger-style holdings may still perform useful economic
functions. The separate roughly 10 percent DeFi-deployment figure remains secondary (SS-073).

A targeted issuer-side check found Securitize's August 12, 2026 results release, now captured. Its
$4.3 billion June 30 AUM is platform-wide, not BUIDL AUM, and is not substituted for the fund
(SS-104). The same release separately quotes approximately $5.0 billion managed onchain;
that description and date basis are not substituted for the June 30 metric. No
current primary BUIDL-specific AUM was established by this continuation.

## 2. Landscape: what is actually established

Total stablecoin market capitalisation was 308.0 billion dollars as of 13 August 2026, up 14.3 percent
year over year, about 99.5 percent dollar-denominated, and 4.5 percent below its May 2026 peak (SS-090).

On this measurement discipline: earlier drafts quoted a 270 to 317 billion dollar range attributed to
Brookings and the Federal Reserve. Neither source is stored as a capture in this record, so **this
draft does not assert that range.** It reports the one aggregator figure it can evidence and names the
single-source limitation. A second independent measurement for the same date is the falsifier.

On issuer reserves, Tether's Q1 2026 attestation by BDO reports about 183 billion dollars of token
liabilities and about 141 billion dollars of direct and indirect US Treasury bill exposure as of March
31, 2026 (SS-040). Three cautions: an attestation is not an audit opinion; the exposure is direct *and
indirect*, so it includes collateral and repo rather than only outright bills; and a detailed
instrument-level breakdown is not in the stored capture.

The primary OCC December 12, 2025 Circle decision is now stored (SS-063). It grants preliminary
conditional approval only and expressly withholds final authorization to commence business until
preopening requirements are met. R3's unqualified approval wording is superseded. Current final
authorization remains UNRESOLVED; a historical conditional order is not evidence of current operation.
The stored CNBC July 10, 2026 article reports later approval under the name Circle National Trust,
direct reserve management and no commercial deposit-taking/lending permission (SS-106). It also
reports a nearly 5 percent share-price rise. This secondary report is accounted for, not ignored;
the entity name differs from the December order and no primary final authorization reconciles
the names or legal acts here. The OCC five-approval announcement separately corroborates the
December conditionality (SS-105); it is not an exhaustive current charter register.

## 3. Open USD: the strongest case, and the falsifiers

What is genuinely documented: a named issuer with a Stripe-company lineage, a live multi-chain launch,
a self-reported reserve page that foots exactly, conditional OCC approval for a trust charter, and an
explicit equity-linked partner incentive structure stated by the company itself (SS-050 through SS-055,
plus SS-052b for the corporate-parent line).

The equity structure is the interesting mechanism and it is the part most likely to be overstated. If
partners earn on supply and activity they drive, then the partners' incentive is volume, and a
volume-seeking distributor has reasons to prefer a stablecoin that pays it over one that does not.
That is a real mechanism, not a slogan. It is also an **inference**, because no partner has publicly
quantified what it receives under either Open Standard's structure or Circle's existing arrangement.

**Hypothesis (SS-101):** partner incentives could increase balances and payment use attributable
to incentivized distributors. Neither supply growth alone nor flat integration count settles this:
existing integrations can absorb more volume. Testing requires partner-level balances, qualifying
payment volume, realized payouts and a credible comparison controlling for launch promotions and
market growth. A comparison finding no incremental partner balances or qualifying use would
undermine the thesis. This record contains no such series; the causal effect remains UNRESOLVED.

**Contrary cases that survive the evidence:**

1. Supply is not adoption. The 468 million dollar OUSD supply snapshot (SS-053) is not a partner-use
   series. Its date and metric differ from the issuer reserve and total-market figures elsewhere
   in this record, so no aligned market-share or causal adoption comparison is asserted.
2. A partner list is intent. It is not routed volume, and nothing in this record measures the gap.
3. Issuer concentration is real despite the governance language. Open Standard describes itself as
   independent while OUSD is issued by Bridge Building Inc., a Stripe company (SS-051, SS-052, SS-052b).
   The independence claim is about the company, not the issuer.
4. The charter is conditional and unverified here (SS-054).

**Narrative, not a finding of superiority:** Open Standard combines partner-oriented reserve
economics with an equity opportunity linked to supply and activity. That may change who benefits
from distribution, but the evidence does not establish that the existing allocation is misallocated
or that the new arrangement is structurally better. Circle already discloses partial Coinbase terms
(SS-064). Comparable contracts and attributable payment use, not a partner list, would distinguish
a material improvement from another distribution arrangement.

## 4. RWA: the depth question

Reported tokenized RWA value excluding stablecoins was about 33.5 billion dollars in early July 2026
per RWA.xyz, against a "represented" or pipeline figure near 345 billion dollars (SS-070). These measure
different things: distributed and freely tradable value versus assets committed to tokenization but not
yet tradable. Conflating them is the single largest error available in this topic, and earlier drafts
did it.

Economic depth is the interesting part. SS-071 now distinguishes the value-weighted 56 percent
from the 70.60 percent zero-transfer count share. Zero weekly transfers is defined in the publisher
summary, but the observation window and full methodology remain unknown. Represented assets make
low transfers an ambiguous signal of utility. The DeFi deployment share remains a single secondary
figure (SS-073), and BUIDL-specific primary AUM remains UNRESOLVED (SS-072).

Two claims earlier drafts made and this draft does not:

- **No economic substitution.** Distinct legal wrappers do not prove RWA and stablecoins do not
  substitute. The mechanism runs both ways, and an earlier report listed substitution mechanisms while
  its bottom line exceeded its evidence.
- **Tokenized fund shares equal asset ownership.** They do not. A tokenized money market fund share is a
  fund interest; the underlying securities are owned by the fund. Legally and economically distinct,
  and the difference matters for every "RWA backing" claim.

**Opinion:** the RWA segment's honest summary from this evidence base is growth in issuance with
unproven depth in use. The 345 billion figure describes commitment, not function.

## 5. Connecting the topics

Each connection below is an **inference** with its competing explanation and falsifier named. None is a
fact, and none should be published as one.

**5.1 Reserve yield, distribution and the payment-incentive layer.**
Circle already pays balance-linked distribution costs (SS-060, SS-061). Open Standard proposes routing
reserve earnings to partners (SS-050). These are the same category of economic arrangement aimed at the
same problem: getting a distributor to prefer your stablecoin. *Competing explanation:* the two differ
materially in rate, in who bears the cost, and in whether partners take issuer risk. This record cannot
distinguish superiority because partial Circle terms (SS-064) do not supply the missing comparable
Open Standard terms. *Test:* comparable net-of-fee contracts and realized partner payments, followed
by attributable use. One disclosed rate alone does not settle the comparison.

**5.2 Gross Treasury holdings versus net new demand.**
Tether's 141 billion dollars of Treasury exposure (SS-040) is gross holdings. The Kansas City Fed's own
analysis (SS-001 through SS-005) illustrates funding-source dependence but has conflicting version
inputs; its positive coefficients are not reliable observed effects. The Fed note also explains
recycling of deposits and foreign-demand effects (SS-006). A bank-account mint is not proof of
deposit substitution: the purchaser may first sell a money-market fund or Treasury. Narrative:
stablecoin growth can redirect short-term safe-asset demand, but issuer purchases alone cannot
measure net new demand or lending contraction. The BIS abstract supplies separate econometric
evidence of short-bill yield effects (SS-007), not a funding decomposition or debt-sustainability
estimate. Following the Fed note's cited research led to the BIS study of monetary shocks and
MMFs (SS-008) and the New York Fed study of within-stablecoin flights to safety (SS-009). Neither
publisher abstract identifies the origin assets financing newly minted stablecoins. Opposite
aggregate responses to a common shock are not purchaser-level funding flows. A separate BIS
macroeconomic paper explicitly models bank-lending and fiscal-space trade-offs (SS-015); it is a
calibrated model, not an observed fiscal dividend. These leads do not close the decomposition.

**Finalisation boundary:** retain the qualitative funding mechanism and separately attributed
yield evidence. Do not estimate market-wide net new Treasury demand, debt relief or lending
contraction. *Test:* holder origin assets, issuance flows, reserve allocations and displaced assets
under a stated counterfactual. Quantitative decomposition remains UNRESOLVED after this targeted
check. Further collection is deferred unless a source containing actual origin-asset data emerges.

**5.3 x402 activity versus stablecoin issuance.**
x402 volume, stablecoin issuance and commerce are three different quantities, and correlation between
minting and payment-rail volume establishes nothing causal. Bridge traffic dominates the x402
headline (SS-081) and automated loops dominate the payment count (SS-082, SS-083). *Competing
explanation:* genuine micropayment commerce may still be growing fast from a small base, which would be
invisible in aggregate value. *Falsifier:* a bridge-excluded, bot-excluded x402 volume series tracked
over time.

**5.4 Governance concentration versus open-infrastructure claims.**
Open Standard describes independent, shareholder-governed governance (SS-051) while issuance sits with
a Stripe subsidiary and the charter is conditional (SS-052, SS-052b, SS-054). Governance of the
*company* and control of the *stablecoin* are separate questions. *Competing explanation:* this may be
the normal shape of regulated stablecoin issuance, where a licensed entity must hold the reserve
regardless of how the ecosystem is governed. *Falsifier:* publication of the actual shareholder and
board structure.

## 6. Regulatory state

GENIUS is enacted with the statutory reserve and disclosure provisions above (SS-010 through SS-013).
Section 20 sets general commencement at the earlier of 18 months after enactment or 120 days after
the primary Federal payment stablecoin regulators issue any final implementing regulations (SS-014).
Section 3(b)(1) separately places its offer/sale prohibition three years after enactment. The
statutory mechanics are established; whether an early final-rule trigger has occurred is UNRESOLVED,
and this record stores no final implementing rule. Do not call every duty currently operative.

Circle's original preliminary conditional order is now stored, not final authorization (SS-063).
Later CNBC approval reporting is separately attributed, with legal continuity unresolved (SS-106).
Bridge's conditional approval remains issuer-reported (SS-054); its original OCC order and current
final authorization remain UNRESOLVED here.

## 8. Honesty boundary

The continuation review is preserved unchanged; its findings were checked against stored sources
and dispositioned in `final/continuation-reconciliation.json`. The resulting corrections have
parent verification, not a second independent review of the revised draft. Passage presence,
hashes, manifest coverage and deterministic builds do not prove semantic support or currentness.

This is a corrected draft assembled from stored captures. It has now been through an independent
challenge (R2, `final-review/challenge.md` and `final-review/findings.json`) and a reconciliation pass
that re-read every accepted finding against the actual capture (`final/review-disposition.json`). The
interrupted Step, DeepSeek and Bunny attempts in this record are still not completed reviews and
nothing here should be read as one. Every claim traces to a stored capture with a hash, and the
passage for each was sliced programmatically from that capture rather than retyped. Where the
evidence does not reach, the label is UNRESOLVED rather than a hedged assertion. Arithmetic is
computed, including the places where computation contradicts a source or an earlier reviewer.

One tooling defect was found and fixed during reconciliation, and it is worth recording because it
shows the verifier can be fooled. The tag-stripping regex used to build capture text was greedy and
treated a literal "<" in body prose as a tag opener. Measured on the stored captures: a table cell
beginning "<$" swallowed 3,716 characters from the Spark Money RWA capture, including the BUIDL
"$2.4 billion as of May 2026" sentence, and a significance marker "<0" swallowed 26,586 characters
from the IMF WP/26/44 capture. Real evidence was invisible until the extractor was made
name-anchored. No claim in the current ledger rests on the broken extraction, but the earlier
figures were built in an environment where the extractor could hide evidence, which is why every
capture is now re-sliced from source at build time.

Nothing in this record establishes operator acceptance, publication approval, or legal compliance.
