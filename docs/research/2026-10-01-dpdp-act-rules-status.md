# India DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025: status as at 1 October 2026

Research brief to support drafting a privacy policy for StableSense that references Indian law
accurately. Research date: **1 October 2026**. Every date, section and URL below is sourced.

**Headline warning for policy drafters.** India's DPDP Act was enacted in August 2023, but it is
**not all in force**. The commencement notification splits the Act into three tranches. As at
1 October 2026 the substantive obligations (notice, consent, security, breach reporting, data
principal rights, penalties) are **not yet in force**; they are scheduled to start in **May 2027**.
Do not write a privacy policy that asserts DPDP obligations are currently binding, and do not cite
the Act's penalty caps as if they apply today. The current operative Indian data protection regime
for most businesses remains the Information Technology Act 2000 and the SPDI Rules 2011 (see
section 8 below).

---

## 1. The DPDP Act 2023: key provisions, rights, obligations

- **Enactment.** The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is **Act No. 22 of 2023**. It
  received Presidential assent on **11 August 2023** and was published in the Gazette of India.
  It runs to **44 sections across 9 chapters**.
  Source: India Code, https://www.indiacode.nic.in/indiacode/handle/123456789/22037?view_type=browse ;
  Act text PDF, MeitY, https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf
- **Section 1(2).** The Act is **not self-executing**. It "shall come into force on such date as the
  Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, appoint and different dates may be
  appointed for different provisions of this Act." Everything about the staggered timeline flows from
  this subsection.
- **Key defined roles (section 2).**
  - **Data Principal** - the individual to whom the personal data relates. For a child, includes the
    parent/lawful guardian; for a person with disability who cannot act independently, the lawful guardian.
  - **Data Fiduciary** - the entity that alone or with others determines the purpose and means of
    processing (analogous to a GDPR controller).
  - **Data Processor** - processes personal data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary (analogous to a processor).
  - **Consent Manager** - a person registered with the Board who provides a single, transparent,
    interoperable platform through which a Data Principal may give, manage, review or withdraw consent.
    This role has no GDPR equivalent.
  - **Significant Data Fiduciary (SDF)** - a Data Fiduciary or class notified by the Central Government
    under section 10, assessed on factors including volume and sensitivity of data and risk to rights.
- **Chapter II - obligations of a Data Fiduciary.**
  - **Section 3** - application/territorial scope (see section 7 below).
  - **Section 4** - processing only for a lawful purpose, with the Data Principal's consent or under a
    "legitimate use".
  - **Section 5** - notice before or with the request for consent: itemised description of personal data,
    purpose, how to exercise rights, how to complain to the Board.
  - **Section 6** - consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with clear
    affirmative action, limited to the specified purpose. Section 6(9) permits consent via a Consent Manager.
  - **Section 7** - the closed list of "legitimate uses" (consent is not the only basis): State functions
    and subsidies/services, employment purposes, legal obligations, medical emergency/epidemic, disaster,
    court judgments, and other notified purposes. There is no open-ended "legitimate interests" balancing
    test (a key GDPR divergence).
  - **Section 8** - general obligations: accountability (8(1)), accuracy (8(3)), reasonable security
    safeguards to prevent a personal data breach (8(5)), breach notification to the Board and each affected
    Data Principal (8(6)), erasure on consent withdrawal or when purpose is no longer served (8(7)).
  - **Section 9** - children's data: **verifiable parental consent for anyone under 18**, prohibition on
    behavioural tracking and targeted advertising directed at children, no processing likely to cause a
    detrimental effect. (Some narrow exemptions in the Fourth Schedule for healthcare, education, child safety.)
  - **Section 10** - additional SDF obligations: India-resident Data Protection Officer, independent data
    audit, DPIA, algorithmic due diligence, and any localisation directions.
- **Chapter III - rights and duties of the Data Principal.**
  - **Section 11** - right to access information about personal data.
  - **Section 12** - right to correction, completion, updating and erasure.
  - **Section 13** - right to grievance redressal.
  - **Section 14** - right to nominate another person to exercise rights on death or incapacity (unique to DPDP).
  - **Section 15** - duties of a Data Principal (no false/frivolous complaints, no impersonation; penalty up to ₹10,000).
  - Note the DPDP gives **four** data principal rights; the GDPR gives eight (access, rectification,
    erasure, restriction, portability, objection, automated-decision safeguards, breach notification).
    DPDP has **no** right to data portability, no right to restriction, no right to object, and no
    right relating to automated decision-making.
- **Section 16** - cross-border transfer. Transfers are **permitted by default**; the Central Government
  may restrict transfers to notified countries/territories (a negative-list model). Sectoral rules survive.
- **Section 17** - exemptions (see section 1 note and section 8 below). Includes 17(1) partial exemptions
  (legal claims, courts, offence investigation, offshore contracts, court-approved mergers, loan default),
  17(2) full- exemption for notified State instrumentalities and for research/archiving/statistics, 17(3)
  startup relief (only on notification), 17(4) State processing carve-outs, 17(5) a five-year power to
  suspend provisions.
- **Chapter V to IX** - the Data Protection Board of India (Board), its powers and procedure, appeals to
  TDSAT, penalties and adjudication, and miscellaneous.

## 2. The DPDP Rules 2025: notification, structure, content

- **Draft Rules.** Published for consultation as **G.S.R. 02(E) on 3 January 2025** (Gazette of India
  Extraordinary, Part II, Section 3(i)). Consultation ran 45 days, later extended (to 5 March 2025 per
  the MeitY feedback summary). **6,915 inputs** per PIB; MeitY's own summary says 6,951 comments.
- **Final Rules.** The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025, notification **G.S.R. 846(E)**,
  Gazette of India Extraordinary, Part II, Section 3(i), **issue No. 760**, notified **13 November 2025**.
  Ten months after the draft. The Rules contain **23 rules and 7 Schedules**.
  Full text (MeitY), https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf
- **The one-day date discrepancy (material, flag it).** The Gazette masthead prints **13 November 2025**
  and the eGazette file code embeds 14112025; the **PIB backgrounder of 17 November 2025 states the Rules
  were notified on 14 November 2025**. Both dates are therefore government-stated. Rule 1 keys the later
  tranches to the *date of publication*, not a calendar date, so the entire downstream schedule shifts by
  one day depending on which reading is used. Practical approach: **plan to the earlier date** (13 Nov 2026,
  13 May 2027), which is correct under either reading.
  PIB backgrounder: https://pib.gov.in/PressReleseDetailm.aspx?PRID=2190655
  PDF: https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf
- **Corrigenda.** **G.S.R. 892(E)**, notified **10 December 2025**, published in Gazette issue No. 806 of
  11 December 2025, corrects G.S.R. 846(E) at 5 Gazette places carrying 8 corrections. Most notably it
  replaces the closing words of Rule 1(3) and Rule 1(4) from "of this Gazette" to "in the Official Gazette".
  It does **not** change any period, obligation or computed date.

### What the Rules require (rules cited in policy drafting)

- **Rule 2** - definitions of "user account", "verifiable consent", "techno-legal measures".
- **Rule 3** - consent notice: standalone, itemised, plain, in English and, on request, in any of the
  22 Eighth Schedule languages; states the personal data, purpose, and how to exercise rights including withdrawal.
- **Rule 4** - registration and obligations of **Consent Managers** (First Schedule: India-incorporated
  company, minimum net worth **₹2 crore**, fiduciary capacity, blind pass-through of data, consent records
  kept at least **7 years**). This is a route for entities that *want* to be Consent Managers; ordinary Data
  Fiduciaries are **not** required to appoint one.
- **Rule 5** - standards where a Data Fiduciary processes on behalf of the State (Second Schedule).
- **Rule 6** - **reasonable security safeguards**: encryption/obfuscation/masking/virtual tokens, access
  controls, logging and monitoring, continuity/backup, processor contract safeguards, and **log and personal
  data retention for at least 1 year** (Rule 6(1)(e)).
- **Rule 7** - **breach notification**: to the Board *without delay* (nature, extent, timing, location,
  likely impact) and a **detailed report within 72 hours** of becoming aware (extendable on written request);
  and to **each affected Data Principal without delay**, in plain language. There is **no materiality/risk
  threshold** (unlike GDPR Art. 34). Separate from, and additional to, the CERT-In 6-hour cybersecurity
  incident duty under the IT Act 2000.
- **Rule 8** - retention and erasure: purpose-end/consent-withdrawal erasure (s 8(7)); a **3-year inactivity
  deemed-purpose-end** for Third Schedule classes (e-commerce ≥2 crore users; online gaming ≥50 lakh;
  social media ≥2 crore), with a **48-hour pre-erasure warning** (Rule 8(2)); and a **minimum 1-year retention**
  of personal data, traffic data and logs for Seventh Schedule purposes (Rule 8(3)).
- **Rule 9** - publish contact information of a Data Protection Officer or other person to answer queries.
- **Rule 10** - verifiable parental consent for children; **Rule 11** - persons with disability;
  **Rule 12** - SDF duties (India-resident DPO, annual DPIA, annual audit, localisation); **Rule 13** -
  additional SDF obligations including algorithmic due diligence; **Rule 14** - Data Principal rights
  machinery, **90-day** response; **Rule 15** - cross-border transfer conditions; **Rule 16** - research
  exemption standards; **Rule 22** - Board procedure; **Rule 23** - calling for information.
- **Schedules.** First (Consent Manager conditions/obligations), Second (State processing standards),
  Third (retention classes/thresholds), Fourth (children's-data exemptions), Fifth (Board service terms),
  Sixth (DPIA/audit categories for SDFs), Seventh (purposes for the 1-year minimum retention).

## 3. Commencement: four instruments of 13 November 2025, three tranches

On **13 November 2025** MeitY published **four** separate instruments. Reading them as one announcement
is the commonest error in secondary coverage.

| Instrument | Job |
|---|---|
| **G.S.R. 843(E)** | Act commencement notification - brings Act provisions into force in 3 tranches |
| **G.S.R. 844(E)** | Establishes the Data Protection Board of India under s 18; head office in the National Capital Region |
| **G.S.R. 845(E)** | Notifies under s 19(1) that the Board shall consist of 4 members |
| **G.S.R. 846(E)** | The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 |

**Tranche 1 - in force from 13 November 2025 (publication).**
- Act: s 1(2), s 2 (definitions), ss 18 to 26 (Board), s 35, ss 38 to 43, s 44(1) and 44(3).
- Rules: **Rules 1, 2 and 17 to 21** (definitions and Board appointment/meeting/digital-office machinery).
- Effect: institutional machinery only. **No substantive data fiduciary obligation is in force.**
  Note that the Board's *powers and functions* under s 27 are **not** in this tranche.

**Tranche 2 - one year from publication (computed 13/14 November 2026, not yet reached).**
- Act: **s 6(9)** (consent via Consent Manager) and **s 27(1)(d)**.
- Rules: **Rule 4** (Consent Manager registration).
- Effect: opens the Consent Manager registration route. Imposes **no** new obligation on an ordinary
  Data Fiduciary.

**Tranche 3 - eighteen months from publication (computed 13/14 May 2027).**
- Act: ss 3 to 5, s 6(1) to (8) and (10), ss 7 to 17, s 27 other than 27(1)(d), **ss 28 to 34**
  (inquiry and penalty regime), ss 36, 37, and s 44(2) (which repeals s 43A of the IT Act).
- Rules: **Rules 3, 5 to 16, 22 and 23** (notice, security, breach, retention, children, disability,
  SDF, rights, cross-border, research, Board procedure, information calls).
- Effect: **this is the compliance line.** Everything a website/app privacy policy would cite arrives here.

Sources: dpdprules.org timeline, https://dpdprules.org/timeline (last verified 6 September 2026);
dpdpindia.in September 2026 briefing, https://dpdpindia.in/monthly-briefing-2026-09.html

## 4. Amendments and developments since enactment

- **Only amending instrument to date: the December 2025 corrigenda (G.S.R. 892(E)).** No notification has
  amended the Act, the Rules, or their commencement.
- **Proposal to shorten 18 months to 12 (January 2026), NOT notified.** Reported by Business Standard on
  22 January 2026 (unnamed sources), discussed at a MeitY stakeholder meeting on 22/23 January 2026; MeitY
  sought comments by 4 February 2026. Reported measures included compressing the main window to 12 months
  (moving the date to ~November 2026), notifying SDFs sooner, and commencing cross-border and
  information-call rules immediately. **No MeitY document about the proposal has been located, and no
  instrument giving effect to it has been notified as at September 2026.** The 18-month schedule therefore
  stands. MeitY Secretary S. Krishnan was reported (14 August 2026) as awaiting industry inputs and
  declining to extend deadlines for startups.
  The 12-month figure applies by the Rules themselves **only to Consent Managers (Rule 1(3))**.
- **Cabinet Secretary directive, 20 August 2026.** Every ministry and state was reportedly ordered to
  produce a time-bound DPDP compliance plan (single-sourced).
- **Board staffing status - treat June 2026 "appointment" claims as unverified.** The Board is legally
  constituted but, on the primary record, **unseated as at late August 2026**: MeitY's circular
  **F. No. 2(1)/2026-Pers.I of 6 May 2026** invited applications for 1 Chairperson and 4 Members (to prepare
  a panel for the Search-cum-Selection Committee), with a follow-up notification on **6 June 2026**; no
  appointment notification had been located. LiveLaw reported on **1 August 2026** that the Board had "no
  appointed Chairperson and no appointed Members." Some low-quality secondary sites claim the Chairperson and
  Members were appointed on 6 June 2026, and Wikipedia names a Chairperson; these are consistent with
  conflating the **6 June 2026 follow-up circular** (about the selection process) with an appointment order,
  and should not be relied on. The Board cannot receive complaints, conduct inquiries or impose penalties
  while unseated, and its s 27 powers are not in force until tranche 3 anyway.
  MeitY circular: https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/05/cd481c027470b420b4cb85fb40a91c53.pdf
- **Supreme Court challenge pending, no stay.** Since February 2026 the Supreme Court has heard writ
  petitions challenging parts of the Act and Rules, led by **Venkatesh Nayak v. Union of India,
  W.P.(C) 177/2026**, and including Reporters' Collective Trust and NCPRI petitions. Provisions under
  challenge include ss 17(1)(c), 17(2), 33(1), 36 and **44(3)** (the RTI Act s 8(1)(j) amendment); Rules 17
  and 23(2). On **16 February 2026** the Court issued notice but **refused an interim stay**. On
  **7 August 2026** it heard argument (bench of CJI Surya Kant, Justice Bagchi, Justice Mohana) and gave the
  Centre two weeks to respond. **Nothing has been struck down or suspended**, so the commencement schedule
  stands. Separately, the Supreme Court on **28 September 2026** asked the Centre to consider statutory rules
  on minors' social media access, noting the DPDP Act's parental-consent provisions but observing they are
  not yet fully in force.

## 5. Key requirements for websites/apps processing Indian users' personal data

When tranche 3 commences (computed 13/14 May 2027), a website or app offering services to users in India will,
as a Data Fiduciary, need to:

1. **Give an itemised, standalone consent notice** before/with each consent request, in English and, on
   request, any of the 22 Eighth Schedule languages (s 5; Rule 3). Bundle nothing.
2. **Obtain free, specific, informed, unconditional, unambiguous consent** by clear affirmative action, or
   rely on a **section 7 legitimate use** (no legitimate-interests balancing).
3. **Maintain reasonable security safeguards** - encryption/masking, access control, logging, backup,
   processor contracts (s 8(5); Rule 6).
4. **Report breaches**: to the Board without delay plus a detailed report within 72 hours, and to every
   affected individual without delay, with no risk threshold (s 8(6); Rule 7). Run this in parallel with the
   CERT-In 6-hour duty.
5. **Honour erasure and retention limits** (s 8(7); Rule 8), including the 48-hour pre-erasure warning and
   the 1-year minimum log/data retention.
6. **Publish a DPO/contact person** (Rule 9) and **respond to rights requests within 90 days** (Rule 14):
   access, correction/erasure, grievance, nomination (ss 11-14).
7. **Handle children's data** with verifiable parental consent and no behavioural tracking/targeted ads
   for under-18s (s 9; Rule 10), and persons with disability (Rule 11).
8. **Cross-border transfer** is permitted by default unless the destination is on a notified restricted list
   (s 16; Rule 15).
9. **Note:** ordinary fiduciaries are **not required to appoint or integrate a Consent Manager**; that
   obligation runs to entities choosing to register as one.
10. **SDFs only** (s 10; Rules 12-13): India-resident DPO, annual DPIA, annual independent audit,
    algorithmic due diligence, possible localisation.

Until tranche 3, Indian-app privacy policies that reference DPDP should describe it as the **incoming**
framework and continue to reference the IT Act/SPDI Rules as the operative law.

## 6. Penalties and enforcement status

The Schedule to the Act sets **maximums per instance**, not fixed fines, imposed by the Board after an
inquiry and a hearing under s 33:

| Breach | Section | Maximum |
|---|---|---|
| Failure to take reasonable security safeguards | s 8(5) | up to ₹250 crore |
| Failure to notify a breach (Board or principals) | s 8(6) | up to ₹200 crore |
| Breach of children's-data obligations | s 9 | up to ₹200 crore |
| Breach of Significant Data Fiduciary obligations | s 10 | up to ₹150 crore |
| Any other breach of the Act or Rules (residual) | - | up to ₹50 crore |
| Breach of a Data Principal's duties | s 15 | up to ₹10,000 |
| Breach of an accepted voluntary undertaking | s 32 | tracks the underlying breach |

Amounts are set case by case using seven factors in s 33(2); penalties go to the Consolidated Fund of India
(s 34). Under s 42 the Government may amend the Schedule but cannot raise any penalty above twice the
enacted amount. Penalties are **fixed rupee maxima, not a percentage of global turnover** (a GDPR divergence).

**Enforcement status as at 1 October 2026: no penalties can be imposed.** Two independent reasons: (a) the
penalty provisions sit in ss 28 to 34, which commence in tranche 3 (computed 13/14 May 2027); and (b) the
Board has, on the primary record, no appointed Chairperson or Members. Expect "soft enforcement" (guidance
and warnings) during 2026 rather than adjudicated fines.

## 7. GDPR comparison and territorial applicability

**Territorial scope (s 3), which is itself not yet in force.**
- s 3(a) - processing of digital personal data **within India**, where collected in digital form or
  non-digitally and digitised later.
- s 3(b) - processing **outside India** where it is "in connection with any activity related to offering of
  goods or services to Data Principals within the territory of India". There is **no establishment
  requirement, no user-count floor, no turnover test**, and free/freemium services count.
- **No "monitoring" limb**, unlike GDPR Art. 3(2)(b). Tracking Indian users' behaviour without offering them
  goods/services is not by itself the statutory hook. Do not import the GDPR monitoring limb by reflex.
- s 3(c) exclusions: (i) personal data processed by an individual for a purely personal/domestic purpose;
  (ii) data made public by the Data Principal herself, or by someone legally obliged to publish it.
  **GDPR has no comparable public-availability carve-out.**

**Material and other differences.**
- **Digital-only.** Paper records that stay on paper are outside the DPDP Act until digitised (s 3(a));
  the GDPR covers structured manual filing systems too.
- **No special category of sensitive data.** The DPDP applies uniformly; no equivalent of GDPR Art. 9.
- **Consent-centric with a closed legitimate-uses list** (s 7); no open legitimate-interests balancing.
- **Four data principal rights vs eight**; no portability, restriction, objection or automated-decision
  rights; adds the uniquely Indian right of nomination.
- **Children:** DPDP sets the age at **under 18** for verifiable parental consent and bans behavioural
  tracking/targeted advertising outright; GDPR sets 13-16 (member-state dependent) and consent-gates rather
  than bans.
- **Breach:** DPDP requires notifying the Board and **every** affected individual with **no** risk threshold;
  GDPR allows skipping individual notice where a breach is "unlikely to result in a high risk".
- **Cross-border:** DPDP permits by default (negative-list restrictions); GDPR requires adequacy or safeguards.
- **Penalties:** fixed rupee maxima per instance (DPDP) vs percentages of global turnover (GDPR).
- **No local-presence duty** in the DPDP Act (no representative, subsidiary or branch requirement). The route
  against a company with no Indian assets is the s 37 blocking power (on a Board reference after monetary
  penalty in two or more instances), which is itself in tranche 3.
- **Overlap with the RTI Act.** DPDP s 44(3) amends s 8(1)(j) of the Right to Information Act. This amendment
  **is in force now** (tranche 1) and is the subject of the pending Supreme Court challenge.

## 8. What applies to Indian users today (1 October 2026)

Because the DPDP Act's substantive obligations are not yet in force, the operative regime for a website or
app processing Indian users' personal data remains:
- the **Information Technology Act, 2000**, including **s 43A** and the **SPDI Rules 2011** (sensitive
  personal data and information); **s 43A and the SPDI Rules are repealed only by s 44(2), which commences
  in tranche 3 (May 2027)**;
- the **CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022** (6-hour cybersecurity incident reporting, 180-day logs stored
  in India), which are in force; and
- any applicable sectoral rules (RBI, IRDAI, SEBI, and so on).

A practical privacy policy for a service with Indian users should therefore: state the current operative
laws accurately; describe the DPDP framework as enacted and progressively commencing, with the substantive
compliance date of May 2027; avoid claiming DPDP obligations are currently binding; and align its
forward-looking commitments with the tranche 3 requirements so it does not need rewriting in 2027.

## 10. Confidence and open items

- **High confidence:** enactment date (11 Aug 2023); Rules notified 13/14 Nov 2025 (G.S.R. 846(E)); the three
  tranches and the Act/Rules provision lists in each; the December 2025 corrigenda; the Schedule penalty
  maxima; the tranche-3 compliance date of ~May 2027; s 3 territorial scope and the GDPR divergences.
- **Open / verify before publishing:** the exact tranche-3 calendar date (13 vs 14 May 2027 - one-day
  masthead vs eGazette discrepancy; Rule 1 sets periods, not dates, so both are computed); whether the Board
  has been seated since late August 2026; and whether the 18-to-12-month acceleration proposal has been
  notified. Re-check MeitY's document library and PIB before the policy is finalised.
